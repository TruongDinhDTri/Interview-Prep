Back-of-the-Envelope Estimation

The mental math toolkit for system design interviews — how to quickly estimate QPS, storage, and latency without a calculator, and why it shapes your architecture decisions.

🔢 Power of 2 ⏱ Latency Numbers ✅ Availability SLA 📈 QPS Estimation

📋 Why Estimation Matters

When an interviewer says "Design Twitter," the very next question should be about scale. Back-of-the-envelope estimation tells you whether one server or a thousand servers are needed — and that answer determines your entire architecture.

🎯 The Goal

You don't need to be precise. You need to be within the right order of magnitude — and you need to communicate your reasoning clearly as you go. Interviewers are watching your thought process, not your arithmetic.

Step 1: AssumptionsState them out loud before calculating
Step 2: CalculateUse round numbers, avoid false precision
Step 3: Conclude"This tells me we need..." — connect to design

⏱ Section 2: Latency Numbers Every Engineer Should Know

Dr. Dean from Google revealed the length of typical computer operations in 2010. Some numbers are outdated as computers become faster, but they still give us an excellent sense of the order of magnitude — the ratio between fast and slow operations is what matters. You don't memorize the exact numbers — you memorize the order of magnitude and the ratio.

OperationLatencyIn NanosecondsMemory Hook
L1 cache reference0.5 ns0.5 ns⚡ Lightning — fastest possible
Branch mispredict5 ns5 ns10x slower than L1 cache
L2 cache reference7 ns7 ns14x slower than L1

Main memory reference (RAM)100 ns100 ns200x slower than L1 ★

Round trip within same datacenter500 µs500,000 ns0.5ms ≈ "local" ★

📏 Unit Reference (memorize this!)

ns = nanosecond  |  µs = microsecond  |  ms = millisecond
1 ns = 10−9 seconds
1 µs = 10−6 seconds = 1,000 ns
1 ms = 10−3 seconds = 1,000 µs = 1,000,000 ns

The 4 Conclusions You Must Be Able to State

🎓 Interview-Ready Insights

1. Memory is fast. Disk is slow. → RAM (100 ns) vs Disk seek (10 ms) = 100,000x difference. Always cache what you can read from disk.

2. Avoid disk seeks. → Use memory-first architectures (Redis, Memcached) for hot data. A disk seek (10ms) costs the same as reading 1MB over the network.

3. Compression beats network transfer. → Compressing 1K bytes costs ~10µs. Sending uncompressed over network costs ~20µs. The CPU overhead of Zippy is worth it — always compress before sending large payloads over the wire.

4. Cross-continent = 150ms minimum. → You cannot beat the speed of light. Global users need CDN edge nodes to reduce this latency.

Key Ratio to Remember: Compression is Cheap vs. Network

💡 The Hidden Insight

Compress 1K with Zippy = 10µs. Send 2K over network = 20µs. This means: compress first, send less. The CPU cost is 2x cheaper than the raw network transfer — and your payload is now smaller so network time also drops. This is why gzip/Brotli on HTTP responses is always on in production.

Using Latency to Justify a Cache Decision

💬 Say This in Interview

"A cache hit is roughly 1ms. A database query hitting disk is roughly 10ms+. With a read-to-write ratio of 10:1, adding a cache reduces average read latency by ~10x. For a URL shortener where the whole value proposition is fast redirects, this is the single most critical optimization."

✅ Section 3: Availability Numbers & SLA

High availability is the ability of a system to be continuously operational for a desirably long period of time. High availability is measured as a percentage, with 100% meaning a service that has 0 downtime. Most services fall between 99% and 100%.

A service level agreement (SLA) is a commonly used term for service providers. This is an agreement between you (the service provider) and your customer, and this agreement formally defines the level of uptime your service will deliver. Cloud providers Amazon, Google and Microsoft set their SLAs at 99.9% or above. Uptime is traditionally measured in nines — the more nines, the better.

As shown in the table below, the number of nines correlates to the expected system downtime:

Availability %Downtime / DayDowntime / YearCalled
99%14.40 minutes3.65 days"Two nines"
99.9%1.44 minutes8.77 hours"Three nines" ★
99.99%8.64 seconds52.60 minutes"Four nines" ★
99.999%864.00 milliseconds5.26 minutes"Five nines"
99.9999%86.40 milliseconds31.56 seconds"Six nines"
💡 How to Count "Nines"

Count the 9s after "99". So 99.9% = three nines, 99.99% = four nines, 99.999% = five nines.

Quick math trick: Each extra nine cuts the downtime by ~10x. Going from 99.9% → 99.99% shrinks yearly downtime from 8.77 hours → 52.6 minutes — a 10x reduction for a huge engineering investment.

Connecting SLA to Architecture

When an interviewer asks "What's the availability requirement?", the answer changes everything:

🏗️ Architectural Implication

99% (3.65 days/yr): Single server acceptable for non-critical systems. No redundancy required. Fine for internal tools.

99.9% (8.77h/yr): Active-passive with manual failover. Single points of failure acceptable in some components. Cost-optimized. Most startups live here.

99.99% (52min/yr): Every component needs redundancy. Active-active load balancers, multi-AZ databases with synchronous replication, automated failover. Significantly higher cost. Required for payments, healthcare.

99.999%+ (5min/yr): Extremely expensive. Requires chaos engineering, global multi-region active-active. Rare — only for life-critical or financial infrastructure.

💬 Say This in Interview

"That's the difference between 8 hours of downtime per year versus 52 minutes — a 10x improvement that requires doubling or tripling infrastructure cost. For a payment system, I'd target 99.99% — for a social feed, 99.9% is probably acceptable and much more cost-effective to engineer. I'd confirm with the product team what the business actually needs before over-engineering."

📈 Section 4: QPS Estimation Framework

QPS (Queries Per Second) is the single most important number in system design because it determines whether you need 1 server or 1,000 servers. Here's the universal formula.

The Core Formula

Average QPS = DAU × requests_per_user_per_day ÷ 86,400 86,400 = 24h × 60min × 60sec. Memorize this number.
Peak QPS = Average QPS × peak_multiplier (typically 2x – 5x) Use 2x for stable traffic, 5x for systems with known traffic spikes (concerts, flash sales)

Full Example: Twitter-like System (from Alex Xu)

📊 Worked Example

Assumptions stated out loud:
• 300M monthly active users, 50% use daily → DAU = 150M
• Each user posts 2 tweets/day, 10% of tweets have media (1 photo ≈ 1MB) 

# QPS Calculation
Tweet QPS = 150M × 2 ÷ 86,400 ≈ 3,500 QPS
Peak QPS  = 3,500 × 2         ≈ 7,000 QPS

# Storage Calculation
Per tweet:        tweet_id(64B) + text(140B) + metadata ≈ 250 bytes
Media tweets/day: 150M × 2 × 10%                        = 30M tweets with media
Daily media:      30M × 1MB                              = 30 TB/day
5-year media:     30TB × 365 × 5                         ≈ 55 PB
💬 Connect to Design

"The media storage dominates at 55PB — text is trivial by comparison at ~137TB over 5 years. This tells me I need blob storage like S3 for media with a CDN. The metadata lives comfortably in a relational database, and I'd shard aggressively for that 7,000 peak QPS write path."

The Universal 6-Step Template

Step 1: DAU = Monthly Active Users × daily usage rate
Step 2: Requests/day = DAU × requests_per_user
Step 3: Average QPS = Requests/day ÷ 86,400
Step 4: Peak QPS = Average QPS × 2 (or 3–5 for spike-prone systems)
Step 5: Storage/day = records/day × size_per_record
Step 6: Storage 5 years = daily × 365 × 5
⚠️ Common Mistake

Calculating QPS after drawing the architecture. Estimation should come first — it informs whether you need a load balancer, database sharding, or a CDN. Estimate first, then design.

💡 Section 5: Interview Tips

Back-of-the-envelope estimation is all about the process. Solving the problem is more important than obtaining the exact result. Interviewers are testing your problem-solving skills. Here are the key tips to follow:

Tip 1: Rounding and Approximation

It is difficult to perform complicated math operations during the interview. There is no need to spend valuable time to solve complicated math problems. Precision is not expected. Use round numbers and approximation to your advantage.

💡 Example

What is the result of 99,987 ÷ 9.1? There is no need to work this out exactly. Simplify it:

→ "100,000 ÷ 10 = 10,000"

The answer is close enough (real answer: ~10,987) and you saved 30 seconds of mental math. In an interview, that time is better spent discussing architecture.

Tip 2: Write Down Your Assumptions

It is a good idea to write down your assumptions to be referenced later. This serves two purposes: it shows the interviewer you are structured, and it lets you catch errors by reviewing your own assumptions.

💬 Say This

"Let me state my assumptions upfront — I'll assume 300M monthly active users, 50% use daily (DAU = 150M), each user posts 2 tweets/day, and 10% of tweets contain a media photo at ~1MB each. I'll work from these numbers — let me know if you want me to adjust any of them."

Writing assumptions gives you a paper trail. If your final number seems off, you can trace it back to the assumption — not recalculate from scratch.

Tip 3: Label Your Units

When you write down "5", does it mean 5 KB or 5 MB? You might confuse yourself with this. Always write down the units — "5 MB" helps to remove ambiguity and shows precision of thought.

⚠️ Common Mistake

❌ "Daily storage: 150M × 2 × 250 = ... some big number"
✅ "Daily storage: 150M users × 2 tweets × 250 bytes/tweet = 75 GB/day"

The unit chain tells you exactly what you computed. Unlabeled numbers are a red flag to interviewers — it signals you may not know what scale you're operating at.

📊 Worked Mini-Example: Number of Servers

Scenario: Peak QPS = 7,000. One commodity web server handles ~1,000 QPS.
Servers needed: 7,000 ÷ 1,000 = 7 servers
With redundancy (N+1): 8–10 servers behind a load balancer.

Say out loud: "That's a manageable fleet — a single availability zone could handle this with a load balancer and auto-scaling group."

Tip 5: Connect Every Number to an Architecture Decision

Every number you calculate should answer a design question. Estimation isn't a math exercise — it's a design decision engine.

🎯 The Bridge

QPS tells you: Do I need a load balancer? How many servers?
Storage tells you: SQL vs NoSQL? Sharding strategy? Cold storage?
Read/write ratio tells you: Cache strategy? Read replicas?
Availability requirement tells you: Multi-AZ? Active-active? What's the failover plan?

💬 Say This — Not Just the Number

"Peak QPS of 7,000 tells me a single database server at ~1,000 QPS capacity won't cut it → we need read replicas and connection pooling for the read path. The write path at 3,500 QPS is also heavy — I'd use async message queuing to absorb spikes and write to the DB in batches."

🎯 Practice Q&A

For each question: close the guide, answer out loud in 2 minutes, then compare with the strong answer below.

Q1: Estimate the QPS for a URL shortener like bit.ly

Weak: "Thousands per second maybe?"

Strong: State assumptions first.
"bit.ly has ~100M monthly active users. 50% use daily → 50M DAU. Each user shortens 1 URL and clicks 10 per day."

Write QPS = 50M × 1   ÷ 86,400 ≈   580 write QPS
Read QPS  = 50M × 10  ÷ 86,400 ≈ 5,800 read QPS
Peak read = 5,800 × 2           ≈ 12,000 QPS

"10:1 read-to-write ratio → optimize the read path with aggressive caching. The top 20% of URLs probably handle 80% of traffic. Cache those in Redis. Write path at 580 QPS is manageable with a single DB + connection pool."

Q2: What are the latency implications of adding a cache?

Weak: "Cache is fast so it'll be faster."

Strong: Quantify it.
"Without cache, every read hits the DB at ~10ms. A cache hit is ~1ms. If our cache hit rate is 95%, the average read latency becomes:"

Average = 0.95 × 1ms + 0.05 × 10ms
        = 0.95  +  0.50
        = 1.45ms (vs 10ms without cache → 7x improvement)

"For a URL shortener where the whole point is instant redirects, this is the most important optimization."

Q3: How much storage would a Twitter-like system need over 5 years?

Strong answer:

"150M DAU, 2 tweets/user/day, 10% with media (1MB photos)."

Text/tweet: tweet_id(64B) + text(140B) + metadata(30B) ≈ 250B
Daily text: 150M × 2 × 250B = 75 GB/day
5yr text:   75GB × 365 × 5  ≈ 137 TB

Media/day:  150M × 2 × 10% = 30M media tweets
Daily media: 30M × 1MB     = 30 TB/day
5yr media:  30TB × 365 × 5 ≈ 55 PB

"Media dominates at 55PB vs 137TB for text. I'd use S3 for media, a relational DB for metadata, and a CDN to serve popular content at edge locations."

Q4: What does "99.99% availability" mean architecturally?

Weak: "Very high availability, nearly no downtime."

Strong: "99.99% means 52.6 minutes of downtime per year — compared to 8.77 hours for 99.9%. Architecturally, 99.99% requires: no single point of failure anywhere, active-active load balancers, multi-AZ database with synchronous replication, automated failover, chaos testing. The cost is significantly higher than 99.9%. I'd push back and ask if 99.9% could work — if it's a payment system, no. If it's a social feed, probably yes."

Q5: Estimate Instagram photo storage over 5 years

Strong:

"500M DAU. Average 1 photo upload per day. After compression: 500KB per photo."

Daily storage: 500M × 500KB = 250 TB/day
5yr storage:   250TB × 365 × 5 ≈ 456 PB ≈ ~0.5 Exabyte

Server count: if 1 server holds 20TB
  → Need 250TB/20TB = 12-13 new servers per day
  → Thousands of servers over 5 years

"This requires distributed blob storage (S3-equivalent), a CDN for reads, and cold storage tiering (Glacier-equivalent) for photos older than 6 months that are rarely accessed."
