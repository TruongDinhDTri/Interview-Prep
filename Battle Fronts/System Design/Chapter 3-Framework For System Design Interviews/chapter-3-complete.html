A Framework for System Design Interviews

No one expects you to design a real-world system in an hour. The final design is less important compared to the work you put in the design process.

"It's about the process, not the answer."
Setting the Stage

Introduction

You have just landed a coveted on-site interview at your dream company. The hiring coordinator sends you a schedule for that day. Scanning through the list, you feel pretty good about it until your eyes land on this session — System Design Interview.

System design interviews are often intimidating. It could be as vague as "designing a well-known product X?". The questions are ambiguous and seem unreasonably broad. Your weariness is well justified. After all, how could anyone design a popular product in an hour that has taken hundreds if not thousands of engineers to build?

The good news is that no one expects you to. Real-world system design is extremely complicated. For example, Google search is deceptively simple; however, the amount of technology that underpins that simplicity is truly astonishing. If no one expects you to design a real-world system in an hour, what is the benefit of a system design interview?

The system design interview simulates real-life problem solving where two co-workers collaborate on an ambiguous problem and come up with a solution that meets their goals. The problem is open-ended, and there is no perfect answer. The final design is less important compared to the work you put in the design process. This allows you to demonstrate your design skill, defend your design choices, and respond to feedback in a constructive way.

Let us flip the table and think about what goes through the interviewer's head as she walks into the conference room to meet you. The primary goal of the interviewer is to accurately assess your abilities. The last thing she wants is to give an inconclusive evaluation because the session has gone poorly and there are not enough signals. What is an interviewer looking for in a system design interview?

Understanding the Game

What Interviewers Really Look For

The system design interview simulates real-life problem solving where two co-workers collaborate on an ambiguous problem.

Many think that system design interview is all about a person's technical design skills. It is much more than that. An effective system design interview gives strong signals about a person's ability to collaborate, to work under pressure, and to resolve ambiguity constructively.

The ability to ask good questions is also an essential skill, and many interviewers specifically look for this skill.

Red Flags

Over-engineering is a real disease of many engineers as they delight in design purity and ignore tradeoffs. They are often unaware of the compounding costs of over-engineered systems, and many companies pay a high price for that ignorance.

Other red flags include narrow mindedness, stubbornness, etc.

The Battle Plan

A 4-Step Process for Effective System Design Interview

Every system design interview is different. A great system design interview is open-ended and there is no one-size-fits-all solution. However, there are steps and common ground to cover in every system design interview.

Step 1 · 3-10 Minutes

Understand the Problem & Establish Design Scope

"Why did the tiger roar?"
A hand shot up in the back of the class.
"Yes, Jimmy?", the teacher responded.
"Because he was HUNGRY".
"Very good Jimmy."
Throughout his childhood, Jimmy has always been the first to answer questions in class. He takes pride in knowing all the answers fast. In exams, he is usually the first person to finish. He is a teacher's top choice for any academic competition.
DON'T be like Jimmy.

In a system design interview, giving out an answer quickly without thinking gives you no bonus points. Answering without a thorough understanding of the requirements is a huge red flag as the interview is not a trivia contest. There is no right answer.

So, do not jump right in to give a solution. Slow down. Think deeply and ask questions to clarify requirements and assumptions. This is extremely important.

As an engineer, we like to solve hard problems and jump into the final design; however, this approach is likely to lead you to design the wrong system. One of the most important skills as an engineer is to ask the right questions, make the proper assumptions, and gather all the information needed to build a system.

When you ask a question, the interviewer either answers your question directly or asks you to make your assumptions. If the latter happens, write down your assumptions on the whiteboard or paper. You might need them later.

Questions to Ask

What kind of questions to ask? Ask questions to understand the exact requirements. Here is a list of questions to help you get started:

  • What specific features are we going to build?
  • How many users does the product have?
  • How fast does the company anticipate to scale up? What are the anticipated scales in 3 months, 6 months, and a year?
  • What is the company's technology stack? What existing services you might leverage to simplify the design?

Step 2 · 10-15 Minutes

Propose High-Level Design & Get Buy-In

Develop a high-level design and reach agreement with the interviewer. Collaborate throughout the process.

  • Come up with an initial blueprint for the design. Ask for feedback. Treat your interviewer as a teammate and work together. Many good interviewers love to talk and get involved.
  • Draw box diagrams with key components on the whiteboard or paper. This might include clients (mobile/web), APIs, web servers, data stores, cache, CDN, message queue, etc.
  • Do back-of-the-envelope calculations to evaluate if your blueprint fits the scale constraints. Think out loud. Communicate with your interviewer if back-of-the-envelope is necessary before diving into it.

If possible, go through a few concrete use cases. This will help you frame the high-level design and discover edge cases you have not yet considered.

Should we include API endpoints and database schema here? This depends on the problem. For large design problems like "Design Google search engine", this is a bit too low level. For a problem like designing the backend for a multi-player poker game, this is fair game. Communicate with your interviewer.

Example: News Feed System

Let us use "Design a news feed system" to demonstrate how to approach the high-level design. Here you are not required to understand how the system actually works. All the details will be explained in Chapter 11.

At the high level, the design is divided into two flows:

Feed publishing: when a user publishes a post, corresponding data is written into cache/database, and the post will be populated into friends' news feed.

Newsfeed building: the news feed is built by aggregating friends' posts in a reverse chronological order.

Figure 3-1 — Feed Publishing (High-Level)
How Feed Publishing Works
1
User publishes a post — The user (from web browser or mobile app) sends a POST request to the API: /v1/me/feed?content=Hello&auth_token={token}. The auth token proves the user's identity so the server knows who is posting.
2
DNS resolution — The request first hits DNS (Domain Name System), which translates the human-readable domain name (e.g., api.facebook.com) into the IP address of the Load Balancer. This is transparent to the user but critical for routing.
3
Load Balancer distributes traffic — The Load Balancer receives the request and routes it to one of the available Web Servers using an algorithm (e.g., round-robin, least connections). This ensures no single server gets overwhelmed and provides high availability.
4
Web Servers fan out to 3 services — The web server authenticates the request, applies rate limiting, then delegates work to three specialized microservices in parallel:
  • Post Service: Responsible for persisting the actual post content. It writes to Post Cache (for fast reads later) and Post DB (for durable storage). This is the source of truth for "what was posted."
  • Fanout Service: The core of feed distribution. It determines which friends should see this post and pushes the post ID into each friend's News Feed Cache. This is what makes the feed appear in real-time for friends.
  • Notification Service: Sends real-time push notifications (mobile push, email, or in-app badges) to alert friends that new content is available. This runs independently — even if notifications fail, the post is still published.
Figure 3-2 — News Feed Building (High-Level)
How News Feed Building Works
1
User opens their feed — The user (web or mobile) sends a GET /v1/me/feed request. Unlike publishing (POST), this is a read-only operation. The goal: show the user a timeline of their friends' recent posts.
2
DNS → Load Balancer → Web Servers — Same routing path as publishing. DNS resolves the domain, Load Balancer picks a healthy Web Server. The Web Server handles authentication (is this a valid user?) and rate limiting (is this user sending too many requests?).
3
Web Servers call News Feed Service — The Web Server delegates to a dedicated News Feed Service, which is the microservice responsible for assembling and returning the user's personalized feed. Separating this into its own service allows independent scaling.
4
Fetch from News Feed Cache — Here's the key insight: the feed was already pre-computed during the publishing step (by the Fanout Service). The News Feed Cache holds a sorted list of post IDs for each user. So this is just a cache lookup — extremely fast, O(1). No expensive computation at read time.
5
Return rendered feed — The assembled feed (with post content, author info, media URLs) is returned to the user's client. The client renders it as the news feed they see on screen. This entire read path is optimized for low latency since users expect feeds to load instantly.
Step 3 · 10-25 Minutes

Design Deep Dive

At this step, you and your interviewer should have already achieved the following objectives:

• Agreed on the overall goals and feature scope

• Sketched out a high-level blueprint for the overall design

• Obtained feedback from your interviewer on the high-level design

• Had some initial ideas about areas to focus on in deep dive based on her feedback

You shall work with the interviewer to identify and prioritize components in the architecture. Every interview is different. Sometimes, the interviewer may give off hints that she likes focusing on high-level design. Sometimes, for a senior candidate interview, the discussion could be on system performance characteristics, likely focusing on the bottlenecks and resource estimations.

In most cases, the interviewer may want you to dig into details of some system components. For URL shortener, it is interesting to dive into the hash function design that converts a long URL to a short one. For a chat system, how to reduce latency and how to support online/offline status are two interesting topics.

Time management is essential as it is easy to get carried away with minute details that do not demonstrate your abilities. Try not to get into unnecessary details.

For example, talking about the EdgeRank algorithm of Facebook feed ranking in detail is not ideal during a system design interview as this takes much precious time and does not prove your ability in designing a scalable system.

Example: News Feed Deep Dive

At this point, we have discussed the high-level design for a news feed system, and the interviewer is happy with your proposal. Next, we will investigate two of the most important use cases:

  1. Feed publishing
  2. News feed retrieval

Figure 3-3 and Figure 3-4 show the detailed design for the two use cases, which will be explained in detail in Chapter 11.

Figure 3-3 — Detailed Feed Publishing
Detailed Feed Publishing Flow
1
Get friend IDs from Graph DB — The Fanout Service first needs to know: "Who are this user's friends?" It queries the Graph DB (a database optimized for relationship data, like Neo4j). For a user with 5,000 friends, this returns all 5,000 friend IDs. Graph DBs are ideal here because traversing relationships (friend-of-friend, mutual friends) is their core strength.
2
Get friends' profile data — With the friend IDs in hand, the Fanout Service fetches each friend's profile from User Cache (with fallback to User DB on cache miss). Why? Because not every friend should receive the post. The system needs to check: Is this friend muted? Have they blocked the poster? Are their notification settings turned off? This filtering step prevents unwanted content delivery.
3
Push to Message Queue — For each eligible friend, the Fanout Service creates a message: <post_id, friend_id> and pushes it into a Message Queue (e.g., Kafka, RabbitMQ). Why a queue? Because if a user has 5,000 friends, writing to 5,000 caches synchronously would be too slow. The queue decouples the "request to fanout" from the "actual fanout work", enabling async processing and retry on failure.
4
Fanout Workers consume messages — A pool of Fanout Workers (stateless servers) pulls messages from the queue. Each worker handles one <post_id, friend_id> pair. Having multiple workers allows the system to scale horizontally — more friends = more workers, not slower processing. If a worker crashes, the message stays in the queue and another worker picks it up.
5
Update News Feed Cache — Each Fanout Worker appends the <post_id> to the friend's personal News Feed Cache entry. The cache stores a sorted list (by timestamp) of post IDs. When the friend later opens their feed (Figure 3-4), this pre-computed list is ready to serve instantly. This is the "write-heavy, read-light" trade-off: we do expensive work at publish time so reads are cheap.
Meanwhile (parallel paths):
• Post Service stores the actual post content (text, media URLs, metadata) into Post Cache (for fast retrieval) and Post DB (for durable storage). This runs independently from the fanout flow.
• Notification Service sends real-time alerts (push notifications, emails, SMS) to friends. Even if notifications fail or are delayed, the post still appears in friends' feeds via the fanout path.
Figure 3-4 — Detailed News Feed Retrieval
Detailed News Feed Retrieval Flow
1
User requests their feed — The user opens the app or refreshes the page, triggering a GET /v1/me/feed request. This is the most latency-sensitive path in the entire system — users expect their feed to appear within milliseconds.
2
Load Balancer → Web Servers — The Load Balancer routes the request to a healthy Web Server. Inside the Web Servers' dashed box, two critical checks happen: Authentication (verify the auth token — is this a real logged-in user?) and Rate Limiting (is this user sending too many requests? Protect against abuse and DDoS).
3
Web Servers call News Feed Service — The Web Server delegates to the News Feed Service, a dedicated microservice whose only job is assembling personalized feeds. This separation of concerns means the feed logic can be scaled, deployed, and optimized independently from the web layer.
4
Fetch post IDs from News Feed Cache — The News Feed Service looks up the user's entry in the News Feed Cache. This cache was pre-populated by the Fanout Workers (from Figure 3-3). It contains a sorted list of <post_id> values — just IDs, not full post data. This keeps the cache compact and fast. The service grabs the top N post IDs (e.g., latest 20 for the first page).
5
Hydration — enrich post IDs with full data — The post IDs alone aren't enough to render a feed. The News Feed Service now "hydrates" each ID by fetching the full details from two sources in parallel:
  • User Cache → User DB: For each post's author, fetch their display name, profile picture URL, verification status, etc. Cache is checked first; on miss, fall back to User DB.
  • Post Cache → Post DB: For each post ID, fetch the actual content: text, image/video URLs, like count, comment count, timestamps. Again, cache first, DB fallback.
6
Return hydrated feed + CDN for media — The fully assembled feed (JSON with author info + post content for each entry) is sent back to the user's client. However, images and videos are not served through the API — the JSON only contains CDN URLs. The client then loads media directly from the CDN (Content Delivery Network), which serves files from edge servers geographically close to the user for minimal latency.
Step 4 · 3-5 Minutes

Wrap Up

In this final step, the interviewer might ask follow-up questions or give you the freedom to discuss additional points.

  • Identify bottlenecks and discuss improvements. Never say your design is perfect and nothing can be improved. There is always something to improve upon. This is a great opportunity to show your critical thinking.
  • Give a recap of your design. This is particularly important if you suggested a few solutions. Refreshing your interviewer's memory can be helpful after a long session.
  • Error cases (server failure, network loss, etc.) are interesting to talk about.
  • Operation issues are worth mentioning. How do you monitor metrics and error logs? How to roll out the system?
  • How to handle the next scale curve. For example, if your current design supports 1 million users, what changes do you need to make to support 10 million users?
  • Propose other refinements you need if you had more time.
Key Takeaway

The wrap-up is your last impression. Use it to demonstrate critical thinking, self-awareness of limitations, and forward-thinking about scaling.

Quick Reference

Dos & Don'ts

☑ Dos

  • Always ask for clarification. Do not assume your assumption is correct.
  • Understand the requirements of the problem.
  • There is neither the right answer nor the best answer. A solution designed to solve the problems of a young startup is different from that of an established company with millions of users.
  • Let the interviewer know what you are thinking. Communicate.
  • Suggest multiple approaches if possible.
  • Once you agree on the blueprint, go into details on each component. Design the most critical components first.
  • Bounce ideas off the interviewer. A good interviewer works with you as a teammate.
  • Never give up.

☒ Don'ts

  • Don't be unprepared for typical interview questions.
  • Don't jump into a solution without clarifying the requirements and assumptions.
  • Don't go into too much detail on a single component in the beginning. Give the high-level design first then drill down.
  • If you get stuck, don't hesitate to ask for hints.
  • Again, communicate. Don't think in silence.
  • Don't think your interview is done once you give the design. You are not done until your interviewer says you are done. Ask for feedback early and often.
