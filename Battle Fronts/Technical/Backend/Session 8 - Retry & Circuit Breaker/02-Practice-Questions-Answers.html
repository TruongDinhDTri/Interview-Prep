Q1 (Very Common): "Service A calls Service B and gets a timeout. Walk me through your complete retry strategy."

🎯

What interviewers want to hear: That you classify the failure first, know the exact backoff formula, understand jitter, and know what not to retry. Bonus points: idempotency awareness.

❌ Weak Answer
❌

"I would retry the request a few times with exponential backoff so we don't overwhelm the service."

Why it's weak: No failure classification, no jitter mention, no idempotency awareness, no stopping conditions. It sounds memorized rather than understood.

🔥 Strong Answer — Step by Step
✅

Structure: Classify → Policy → Jitter → Stop conditions → What NOT to retry

Step 1️⃣ — Start with diagnosis: what kind of failure is this?

"First, I need to classify the failure. A timeout falls into 'transient' — it could be a network blip, a brief overload, or a GC pause on Service B. These typically self-resolve within seconds, so retrying is appropriate. But I need to know: was this request a GET (safe to retry) or a POST that creates a resource? Retrying a non-idempotent POST without an idempotency key creates duplicate records."

Step 2️⃣ — The retry policy with exact numbers

"Assuming the operation is idempotent — or we've assigned an idempotency key — here's the policy: I would NOT retry immediately. The first retry waits 100ms–1s, the second waits 2–4s, the third waits 4–8s. Each wait time adds random jitter — not exactly 1s, but somewhere in [0, 1s]. This is critical: if 500 Service A instances all timed out at the same moment, without jitter they'd all retry at exactly T+1s, creating a synchronized 500× request spike on an already-struggling Service B."

Step 3️⃣ — Stopping conditions

"Maximum 3–4 retries. If all 4 attempts fail, I stop and propagate the error up the stack. Retrying more than that on a service that's genuinely down just wastes CPU and holds connections. The circuit breaker — if configured — will open after the first few failures anyway, switching future calls to fail-fast mode."

Step 4️⃣ — What I would NOT retry

"If Service B returns a 400 Bad Request instead of a timeout, I do not retry — my request payload is malformed, and retrying the same bad payload is pointless. Same for 401/403 — auth/permission errors don't resolve with time. Retrying those adds load for zero benefit."

💡

Key phrase to drop: "Retry only idempotent operations, only on transient errors, with exponential backoff and full jitter, capped at 3-5 attempts."

Q2: "What is the thundering herd problem and how does jitter solve it?"

🧠

What interviewers want: Concrete numbers. Draw the mental picture of 2,000 instances all retrying at the same millisecond. Then explain exactly how jitter distributes that spike.

❌ Weak Answer
❌

"Thundering herd is when too many requests hit a service at once. Jitter adds randomness to spread them out."

Why it's weak: No concrete scenario, no numbers, no explanation of how jitter actually works or why full jitter is better than equal jitter.

🔥 Strong Answer — Concrete Numbers Required
⚡ Set up the scenario with exact numbers first

"Let me make this concrete. Imagine 2,000 Order Service instances all share the same Payment Service. At T=0, Payment Service experiences a brief overload spike and returns 503 to all 2,000 instances simultaneously. Each instance uses exponential backoff and waits exactly 1 second before retrying."

💥 Describe the thundering herd cascade

"At T=1 second: all 2,000 instances retry simultaneously. Payment Service, which was just starting to recover from the T=0 overload, is now hit with a synchronized 2,000-request spike. This is 2–5× its normal load. It overloads again. All 2,000 instances get another failure. At T=2s: another synchronized spike of 2,000 requests. The system is caught in a retry loop where the retries are causing the very overload they're trying to recover from. This is the thundering herd — the herd of retries stampeding in unison."

🆕 How jitter breaks the synchronization

"Jitter destroys the synchronization by making each instance wait a different amount of time. With full jitter, instead of everyone waiting exactly 1 second, each of the 2,000 instances waits a random duration between 0 and 1 second. The retry load that was 2,000 requests at a single point in time is now ~2,000 requests spread across a 1-second window — about 2 requests per millisecond. That's 500× less peak load. Payment Service experiences a smooth drizzle of retries instead of a tidal wave. It recovers, retries succeed, system stabilizes."

💡

Implementation detail interviewers love: AWS, Google, and Netflix all recommend full jitter — random.uniform(0, exponential_max) — rather than adding jitter to a fixed base ("equal jitter"). Full jitter provides the best load distribution at scale because it makes the wait truly random, not just shifted by a random amount.

Q3: "Explain circuit breaker states and when each transition happens."

🎯

What interviewers want: The analogy, exact transition triggers, why half-open exists (and why you don't jump straight to closed), and how failure counting works.

❌ Weak Answer
❌

"Circuit breaker has three states: closed, open, and half-open. It opens when there are too many failures and closes when the service recovers."

Why it's weak: Correct but surface-level. No transition triggers, no why half-open is needed, no sliding window detail, no implementation awareness.

🔥 Strong Answer — Analogy First, Then Precision
⚡ Start with the analogy to ground the concept

"The name comes from household electrical circuit breakers — the safety device that trips and disconnects a circuit when it detects dangerous conditions, preventing fires. Same idea in software: detect a dangerous pattern (too many failures), disconnect the circuit (stop sending requests), test recovery, reconnect when safe."

🟢 CLOSED state — the baseline with sliding window detail

"In the CLOSED state, all requests flow through normally. The circuit breaker counts failures in a sliding time window. The specific threshold is configurable — a common setting is '50% failure rate over the last 10 requests' or '5 consecutive failures.' I prefer rate-based thresholds over count-based ones: a service with 10 requests/minute behaves very differently from one with 10,000 requests/minute, but a 50% failure rate is meaningful in both cases."

🔴 CLOSED → OPEN transition & what OPEN does

"When the failure threshold is breached — say, 6 out of the last 10 calls failed — the circuit transitions to OPEN immediately. From this point, every incoming request fails instantaneously without making a network call. The caller receives a CircuitBreakerOpenException in microseconds instead of waiting for a network timeout. Threads freed immediately. Recovery timer starts. This is exactly what we want: instead of 100 threads piling up waiting 30 seconds each for a dead service, they all fail in microseconds."

🟡 HALF-OPEN — why it exists and how it works

"After the recovery timeout expires — usually 30–60 seconds — the circuit doesn't immediately go back to CLOSED. It enters HALF-OPEN: a cautious testing mode. A small number of probe requests — 1 to 3 — are allowed through. This avoids the scenario where the service barely recovers and immediately getting hit with full traffic causes it to crash again.

If the probe requests succeed: CLOSED — full traffic resumes. If they fail: back to OPEN — another recovery timeout begins. This can cycle a few times if the service is intermittently recovering — normal and expected."

💡

Key phrase for why not jumping straight to CLOSED: "Half-open is conservative: one success per second, not full traffic. If truly recovered, CLOSED within seconds; if not, back to OPEN safely."

Q4: "You need to retry a POST request that creates an order. What could go wrong, and how do you handle it?"

🎯

What interviewers want: You must describe the exact failure mode precisely (not just "duplicates happen"), then explain idempotency keys with the specific server-side logic, and ideally reference Stripe as a real-world example.

❌ Weak Answer
❌

"POST requests aren't idempotent so retrying could create duplicate orders. We should use an idempotency key."

Why it's weak: Correct direction but no depth. Doesn't explain the exact failure scenario, doesn't explain what an idempotency key actually does on the server side, and no TTL awareness.

🔥 Strong Answer — The Exact Failure Mode First
⚠️ Articulate the precise failure scenario

"Here's the precise failure mode: the client sends POST /orders with the order data. The Order Service receives it, processes it, creates the order in the database, and sends back a 201 Created response. But at exactly that moment — network congestion — the response packet is dropped. The client sees a timeout: no response within 5 seconds. From the client's perspective: the request failed. From the server's perspective: the order was created successfully.

If the client naively retries the POST, the Order Service creates a second order — same items, same user, same amount. The user is now charged twice and has two orders. This is a data integrity disaster."

🔑 The solution — idempotency keys, server-side logic

"The standard solution is an idempotency key: the client generates a unique UUID for this operation before sending the first request. It includes this key in every retry of the same operation. The server stores (idempotency_key, response) after processing. On receiving a request, the server first checks: have I already processed this idempotency key? If yes, return the stored response without re-processing. If no, process and store."

Client:
  idempotency_key = uuid4()  # Generated ONCE, reused for all retries

  POST /orders
  Headers: Idempotency-Key: "abc-123-xyz-def"
  Body: { product: 456, quantity: 1 }

  [timeout ➞ retry]

  POST /orders
  Headers: Idempotency-Key: "abc-123-xyz-def"  ← same key
  Body: { product: 456, quantity: 1 }

Server:
  Request 1: key "abc-123-xyz-def" not seen ➞ create order ➞ store key+response
  Request 2: key "abc-123-xyz-def" already seen ➞ return stored 201 ➞ no new order
💎 Stripe reference + TTL consideration

"Stripe is the canonical example — every charge request accepts an Idempotency-Key header. Their documentation explicitly states: always include this for payment operations, and reuse the same key for retries of the same charge.

One implementation detail: the idempotency key storage needs a TTL — you don't want to store every key forever. Stripe uses 24 hours — long enough to cover any reasonable retry window. If a client retries after 25 hours, they might get a duplicate, but that's an extreme edge case far beyond normal retry scenarios."

💡

Key phrase: "Same key in every retry — server returns stored result if key seen before. This makes POST effectively idempotent."

Q5: "How do bulkheads prevent cascading failures? Give me a concrete example."

🎯

What interviewers want: The ship analogy (briefly), then a concrete before/after scenario with real numbers showing exactly how thread exhaustion propagates without bulkheads, and how it's contained with them.

❌ Weak Answer
❌

"Bulkheads isolate different services so that if one fails, it doesn't affect the others."

Why it's weak: Conceptually correct but no mechanism, no numbers, and no concrete example. Interviewers can't assess whether you actually understand it.

🔥 Strong Answer — Numbers and Mechanism
⚓️ The analogy — briefly but effectively

"The name comes from ship design — watertight compartments that prevent one breach from flooding the whole vessel. In software, it's the same idea: isolate failure domains so a breach in one doesn't sink everything."

💥 Without bulkheads — the failure cascade

"Concrete scenario. Order Service has a single thread pool of 100 threads for all outbound service calls. It calls three services: Payment Service (normally 50ms), User Service (normally 20ms), and Inventory Service (normally 30ms).

Payment Service starts responding in 5 seconds due to a database issue. Order Service handles 50 requests/second. At 50 requests/second to Payment Service, each taking 5 seconds: threads are occupied for 5 seconds each. Within 2 seconds: 100 threads are all full, waiting for Payment responses. Now a User Service call comes in — no threads available, it queues. An Inventory call — queues. Within 10 seconds, the request queue is full. Order Service is returning errors for everything — not just payment operations, but user lookups, inventory checks, everything. One slow downstream service has made our entire Order Service unresponsive."

✓ With bulkheads — blast radius contained

"With bulkheads, Order Service has three separate thread pools:

  • Payment pool: 30 threads
  • User pool: 30 threads
  • Inventory pool: 30 threads

Same scenario: Payment Service becomes slow (5s). Payment pool's 30 threads fill up within 1 second. New payment requests queue and then fail when the queue is full. But User Service calls? Still have 30 dedicated threads — completely unaffected. Inventory calls? Same — 30 dedicated threads untouched.

The blast radius is 'payment-related requests are failing.' All other Order Service functionality is unimpaired. Users can still look up their account, view inventory, do everything except pay."

💡

Key insight to close with: "Bulkheads don't prevent failures — they contain them. The goal isn't to make Payment Service fast again; it's to ensure that when Payment fails, it's the only thing that fails." In large microservices systems, complete availability is impossible — partial degradation is the best you can achieve, and bulkheads make partial degradation the outcome instead of total failure.

Q6: "The circuit to your Payment Service opens. What does the user experience? What should the system do?"

🎯

What interviewers want: Three levels of user experience (worst → best), the operational response beyond just UX (alerting, queuing), and the principle that failure behavior should be designed in advance, not discovered during an incident.

❌ Weak Answer
❌

"The user will see an error. We should show a friendly error message and maybe use a fallback."

Why it's weak: No infrastructure-level explanation, no levels of fallback quality, no operational response (alerting, queuing), no design-time principle.

🔥 Strong Answer — Infrastructure to UX to Operations
⚡ First: What circuit open means at the infrastructure level

"When the circuit opens, every call to Payment Service returns a CircuitBreakerOpenException in microseconds — no network round-trip. From the user's perspective, the checkout page responds immediately — but with an error or degraded state, depending on how we've designed the fallback."

🌟 Three levels of user experience — worst to best
1

Level 1 (worst): Generic 500 error page

❌

'Something went wrong.' No context, no next steps. User leaves, possibly never returns. This is what happens with no fallback logic.

2

Level 2 (acceptable): Informative error message

⚡

Instead of a generic crash: "Payment processing is temporarily unavailable. Your cart has been saved — please try again in a few minutes." Requires the UI to handle the payment error gracefully, not propagate it as a full page crash. Critically, the user's cart state is preserved.

3

Level 3 (best for critical path): Queue and notify

✓

Accept the order intent, queue it, and process when Payment Service recovers. Show user: "We're processing your order — you'll receive confirmation by email within 5 minutes." Requires the ability to decouple order acceptance from payment processing. Works for some businesses, not all.

📊 What the SYSTEM should do — beyond UX

"The system should immediately alert on-call engineers when the circuit opens — this isn't a normal state, it indicates a real incident with Payment Service. The circuit state should be visible in the monitoring dashboard alongside the circuit open time and recovery timeout countdown.

Ideally, there's also a queue of failed payment attempts that can be retried when the circuit closes. For e-commerce, losing payment attempts during a 30-minute outage can mean significant revenue loss — queuing them for automatic retry when the circuit recovers is important."

💎

Closing principle that impresses interviewers:

"Every circuit opening should have a defined user experience and operational response designed in advance — not discovered in the middle of an incident. 'What happens when X fails?' should be answered at design time, not at 3am during an outage."
