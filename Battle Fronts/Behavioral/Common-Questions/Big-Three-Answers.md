# The Big Three — Most Critical Behavioral Questions

These three questions appear in nearly **every** behavioral interview. Your answers must be polished, natural, and well-timed.

---

## 1. "Tell me about yourself" (30 seconds)

> Hi, I'm Tri — a backend-focused software engineer with 2-3 years of experience, primarily in **Python/Django** and **JavaScript/Node.js**. I'm passionate about building tools that multiply team productivity.
>
> At my current company, I built **Jarvis-Bot**, a serverless Slack automation that eliminated 3,120 hours of manual work per year for a team of 20+. I also engineered a **Chrome extension** that cut our dev-test cycle from 30 minutes to 30 seconds by hot-swapping production scripts locally.
>
> What excites me most is **AI-native development** — I built a personal RAG-powered knowledge retrieval system that 10 colleagues adopted. I'm looking for a role where I can bring this builder mindset to a team that values engineering craft and measurable impact.

**Key points:**
- Name + role + stack (5 sec)
- 2 impressive metrics (15 sec)
- What excites you + what you're looking for (10 sec)
- **Total: ~30 seconds**

---

## 2. "Tell me about your favorite / most impactful project" (3 minutes)

**Use Story 1: Jarvis-Bot** (for impact) or **Story 3: Second Brain** (for innovation)

### Jarvis-Bot Version (Impact-focused):

> **Situation** (15 sec): At my company, a team of 20+ developers spent 3 hours every week manually creating timesheets — cross-referencing Slack messages with project codes and formatting Japanese-standard CSVs.
>
> **Task** (10 sec): I calculated this was wasting 60+ engineering hours weekly. I set out to automate it completely while keeping 100% accuracy.
>
> **Action** (90 sec): I proposed and built "Jarvis-Bot" — a serverless Slack bot using Python and Firebase Functions. The core challenge was building an NLP parser that could extract task codes from conversational Slack threads. I integrated Google Drive and Sheets APIs for the data pipeline, implemented UTF-8-BOM encoding for Japanese characters, and aggressively optimized cloud costs by tuning memory allocation, filtering events to only `app_mention`, and adding exponential backoff for reliability.
>
> **Result** (15 sec): The process went from 3 hours to 30 seconds. We saved 3,120 hours annually — over $240k in productivity value. It became the team standard and was a key factor in my salary increase.
>
> **Reflection** (30 sec): This crystallized a principle I now apply everywhere: "Measure the invisible tax." Teams accept painful manual work because no one quantifies the cost. That single calculation — 3,120 hours — is what turned a "nice to have" into an "urgent priority." Now, whenever I see a repetitive workflow, my first instinct is to measure its true cost.

## 3. "Tell me about a time you dealt with conflict" (3 minutes)

**Use Story 4: The Conflict**

> **Situation** (15 sec): During a casual work conversation, my team leader — under severe deadline pressure — suddenly stood up and shouted personal insults at me in front of the entire office, including interns and the CTO.
>
> **Task** (10 sec): I was humiliated and furious. But I knew that retaliating would damage team culture and my professional reputation. I needed to address this firmly but strategically.
>
> **Action** (90 sec): In the moment, I chose complete de-escalation — silence, return to work, demonstrate control. Then I waited for my formal performance review with the CEO to raise the issue in the proper forum. When the leader tried to excuse his behavior as "stress" or "just my personality," I pushed back clearly: "Stress is not an excuse for disrespect. I value our working relationship, but I don't accept personal insults. I expect an apology because professional standards demand it."
>
> **Result** (15 sec): The CEO backed me immediately. The leader apologized. The behavior never repeated. I established myself as someone who demands respect without losing composure.
>
> **Reflection** (30 sec): This taught me that professional boundaries aren't optional — they're infrastructure, like error handling in code. And the *timing* of confrontation matters as much as the content: addressing it in the formal review gave me credibility and leverage. Now in any new team, I proactively establish communication norms early — it's preventive engineering for team culture.
